# Introduction
Life expectancy serves as a fundamental measure of a nation’s overall well-being, reflecting the combined impact of healthcare systems, economic development, and social policies. While prior research has established that both economic and social factors influence longevity, the extent and nature of these relationships vary significantly across different national contexts. Some high-income nations still experience health disparities, while certain lower-income countries demonstrate unexpectedly high life expectancy. Understanding these patterns is crucial for shaping effective public health and economic policies. Therefore, this study seeks to examine how economic factors, such as GDP and health expenditure, alongside social factors like education, BMI, and country status, influence life expectancy. We can hypothesize that higher GDP per capita, increased health expenditure, and better education levels positively influence life expectancy across different countries, while higher BMI and lower country development status negatively impact life expectancy.

Studies (Chen et Al, 2021) show that  GDP per capita is the most significant factor positively influencing Life expectancy in developing countries. However, it argues that increased spending on healthcare has not translated into better health outcomes, indicating a need for more effective strategies. For developing countries, the correlation is not significant. Education significantly benefits health outcomes and Life expectancy. However, the role of education in developed countries isn't explicitly detailed in the provided data, reflecting a need for further research into its impact. Research (Moga et Al, 2022) also shows that educational attainment is found to have a greater effect on life expectancy. The study on BMI (Preston, 2011) shows that the high levels of obesity in the United States are a major contributor to its lower international life expectancy. A Linear regression model is good for this research question because it helps identify whether the relationships align with prior research. If GDP, for example, has a strong positive effect on life expectancy in developing countries but not in developed ones, the model can reveal these distinctions. Therefore, our model aims to be descriptive to explore patterns in existing data.

## Data Description

This study utilizes the "Life Expectancy Data" compiled from the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations, encompassing country-level indicators between 2000 and 2015. The dataset integrates health, economic, and demographic variables sourced from national health surveys, government reports, and international organizations. Health-related variables, including life expectancy, BMI, and healthcare expenditure, were primarily derived from WHO databases, while economic indicators such as GDP and education levels were obtained from World Bank and national census sources. The dataset was originally curated to explore the impact of immunization coverage, healthcare spending, and socioeconomic conditions on life expectancy across different income groups. For the purpose of this research, the analysis is restricted to examining the relationship between selected social determinants and life expectancy. The choice of predictors is guided by empirical findings from prior studies (Chen et al., 2021; Moga et al., 2022; Preston & Stokes, 2011), which have consistently demonstrated strong associations between life expectancy and variables such as national income, education, and healthcare investment.

The outcome variable, life expectancy (in years), is a continuous measure representing the average expected lifespan under current mortality conditions. It is well-suited to linear regression analysis due to its distributional properties and theoretical links with the selected predictors. Specifically, we hypothesize that GDP, education, and total healthcare expenditure will be positively associated with life expectancy. Additionally, BMI is expected to exhibit a curvilinear relationship, where moderate levels reflect adequate nutrition, while extreme values (underweight or obese) may negatively influence health outcomes.

## Model Selection

To improve the model's performance and better satisfy the linear regression assumptions, we applied a series of transformations to key predictors. Specifically, income_composition, total_expenditure, gdp, and alcohol were first log-transformed to reduce skewness and mitigate non-linearity. However, the statistical significance of log_gdp was still not optimal in the initial model. Therefore, we further adjusted log_gdp by applying a square-root transformation, resulting in log_gdp_sqrt, which yielded a more significant predictor. With this approach, all variables in the final model reached the required levels of statistical significance improving the explanatory power of the model.
Following these transformations, we assessed the presence of multicollinearity by calculating the Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) for each predictor. All VIF values were below the commonly accepted threshold of 5, indicating no severe multicollinearity among the explanatory variables. This suggests that the predictors are not highly linearly related to one another, thus supporting the stability of coefficient estimates and the reliability of interpretations. Additionally, the transformations contributed to improved model assumptions. The residuals versus fitted values plot demonstrated more constant variance, while the linearity between predictors and the response variable was enhanced. Normality of residuals also improved, with only minor deviations observed in the Q-Q plot’s tails. The histogram of standardized residuals further confirmed this, showing a symmetric and bell-shaped distribution. Altogether, these transformations produced a more robust model with better inference quality and predictive performance.
In this model, a diagnostic analysis revealed 4 outliers, 176 high leverage points, and 150 influential observations, based on studentized residuals, hat values, and Cook’s distance, respectively. The model uses real-world data to predict life_expectancy based on variables like gdp, status, total_expenditure, alcohol, and income_composition, among others, which naturally reflect substantial global differences across countries. Despite identifying these statistical anomalies, a deliberate decision was made not to remove any of them. All data points are drawn from actual national statistics, each representing unique socioeconomic contexts. The presence of extreme values—such as extremely wealthy or underdeveloped countries—is expected in a dataset of this nature. Although these points may violate certain model assumptions or exert influence, they are not data errors. Instead, they provide valuable insights into the diversity of global development. Removing them would reduce the model’s explanatory power and compromise its ability to capture real-world variation. To preserve analytical integrity and ensure meaningful interpretation across all global contexts, all observations were retained in the final analysis.
In the model development process, BMI and schooling were excluded from the final regression due to consistent statistical and diagnostic concerns. Despite multiple transformation attempts—including log and square-root transformations—these variables failed to reach statistical significance. Additionally, their inclusion did not lead to noticeable improvements in satisfying key model assumptions, particularly regarding constant variance and the normality of residuals. In fact, the histogram of standardized residuals remained skewed, and Q-Q plots continued to show deviation from the theoretical line. Given these issues, retaining bmi and schooling would have introduced noise and undermined model interpretability. To address this, I replaced them with more theoretically and empirically robust predictors: income_composition and alcohol. Both of these variables are directly tied to health-related outcomes and demonstrated strong statistical significance after log transformation. Their inclusion not only improved model fit but also better supported the linearity and normality assumptions, contributing to a more reliable and interpretable regression model.

## Final model inference and results

The final linear regression model utilizes log-transformed predictors to thoroughly investigate the relationship between key socioeconomic indicators and national life expectancy. This methodological choice enhances interpretability by normalizing variable distributions, thus facilitating comparisons across different scales and countries. The comprehensive model demonstrates a strong explanatory capacity, accounting for approximately 79% of the variance in life expectancy (Adjusted R² = 0.79). Such a high explanatory power highlights the model's robustness, confirming its suitability for analyzing global health outcomes.
Each predictor in the regression model represents an internationally acknowledged determinant influencing health outcomes, grounding the analysis firmly within global public health discourse. A detailed interpretation of the regression coefficients reveals nuanced insights into how each socioeconomic and behavioral factor uniquely affects life expectancy.
Firstly, the binary variable StatusDeveloping, which classifies countries into developed or developing categories, exhibits a significantly negative coefficient (-2.13, p < 0.001). This finding explicitly indicates that developing nations, when other factors remain constant, have an average life expectancy 2.13 years lower compared to their developed counterparts. This statistically significant disparity highlights entrenched global health inequalities. It further emphasizes the persistent gap in healthcare access, nutritional status, educational opportunities, and overall living conditions between developed and developing nations. The result aligns with existing literature on global health inequities, emphasizing the need for targeted international development policies and healthcare interventions specifically tailored to improving health outcomes in lower-income regions.
The predictor log_Income, representing the logarithmic transformation of the Human Development Index's income composition sub-index, emerges as the strongest determinant, carrying a highly significant positive coefficient (30.29, p < 0.001). This indicates that a 1% increase in the income composition sub-index, reflecting improvements in education, income, and living standards, corresponds to roughly a 0.30-year increase in life expectancy. Given that this sub-index encapsulates broader socioeconomic development, its prominent effect underscores the pivotal role of economic and educational progress in enhancing population health. This relationship supports the broader socioeconomic theory that improved economic status facilitates better access to quality healthcare, adequate nutrition, and health literacy, thereby significantly extending life expectancy.
Moreover, the variable log_Expenditure, the logarithmic transformation of government health expenditure expressed as a percentage of total government expenditure, shows a positive and statistically significant relationship with life expectancy (0.74, p < 0.001). This coefficient suggests that a 1% increase in government spending dedicated to healthcare is associated with an incremental rise of approximately 0.0074 years (approximately 2.7 days) in life expectancy. Although this increment appears modest, its statistical significance implies that incremental increases in healthcare investment can cumulatively contribute to meaningful improvements in national health outcomes. This finding highlights the critical policy implication that prioritizing healthcare budgets can yield measurable and beneficial public health outcomes, advocating for sustained or enhanced health-sector financing.
Interestingly, log_GDPrsq, calculated as the square root of the logarithmic transformation of GDP per capita, shows a slightly negative coefficient (-0.77, p < 0.05), which is indicative of diminishing marginal returns. This result aligns well with prevailing economic theories suggesting that, while economic growth generally promotes better health outcomes, there are limitations to its effectiveness beyond certain thresholds. At higher levels of GDP per capita, the incremental health benefits from additional economic gains reduce markedly, possibly due to heightened inequality, inefficiencies in resource allocation, or saturation of essential healthcare needs. This insight suggests that policymakers should strategically balance economic growth objectives with equitable and effective distributional policies, ensuring that health benefits derived from economic prosperity are maximally realized across all societal sectors.
The variable log_Alcohol, representing the logarithmic transformation of per capita alcohol consumption (litres of pure alcohol annually among individuals aged 15 and older), also demonstrates a significantly negative coefficient (-0.40, p < 0.001). This signifies that a 1% increase in alcohol consumption correlates with a reduction in life expectancy by approximately 0.004 years (about 1.5 days). Although the magnitude appears small, its statistical significance reiterates the well-established detrimental effects of alcohol consumption on public health. This result aligns with extensive epidemiological evidence linking alcohol use to numerous chronic diseases and premature mortality, reinforcing the importance of robust public health policies targeting reductions in harmful alcohol use through education, regulation, and intervention strategies.
The model revealed several key findings regarding the determinants of national life expectancy, highlighting both consistencies and divergences with existing literature. One of the most notable results was the significantly lower life expectancy in developing nations compared to developed ones, emphasizing the persistent global health inequalities. This aligns with research by Chen et al. (2021), which found that in developing countries, life expectancy positively correlates with urbanization rates, likely due to increased access to infrastructure and healthcare services in urban areas. Our analysis also found several variables that closely mirrored prior research. For instance, government health expenditure was positively associated with life expectancy, although the effect was modest. This finding supports Moge et al. (2022), who argue that improvements in healthcare systems and medical care access have contributed to rising life expectancy globally. They further note that economic freedom can enhance healthcare accessibility and longevity, though its benefits may be curtailed if not balanced with environmental regulations. Alcohol consumption was another influential factor, with our results indicating a negative correlation with life expectancy. This is consistent with findings from Ranabhat and Kim (2020), who documented how excessive alcohol use contributes to a reduction in average lifespan due to its health-related consequences. In terms of notable differences, our research identified log_Income (an income composition sub-index) as the strongest predictor of life expectancy. This suggests that increases in income composition—reflecting not just raw income but equitable distribution and accessibility—can significantly enhance life outcomes. Interestingly, while GDP per capita was positively associated with life expectancy at lower levels, the relationship plateaued at higher levels, indicating diminishing returns. This diverges from Chen et al. (2021), who reported a positive correlation in developed countries but a negative one in developing countries, challenging the assumption that economic growth uniformly improves health outcomes.
To validate the robustness and effectiveness of the final model, we rigorously compared its performance with the initial model proposed, which utilized untransformed predictors. Multiple statistical evaluation metrics were employed in this comparative analysis, including the R-squared, Adjusted R-squared, and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The original model explained approximately 61.93% of the variation in life expectancy (Adjusted R-squared of 0.6185), reflecting moderate explanatory power. In contrast, the final model, employing logarithmic transformations for several variables and a square-root transformation specifically for GDP per capita, achieved a substantial improvement, explaining 79.05% of the variance (Adjusted R-squared = 0.79). This marked enhancement clearly demonstrates the superior predictive accuracy of the transformed model.
Moreover, the AIC, which balances model complexity against goodness-of-fit, decreased significantly from 14827.35 in the original model to 12897.47 in the final transformed model. This substantial reduction further confirms that the final transformed model provides a more efficient and statistically sound explanation of life expectancy variability across nations.
In conclusion, the refined regression model effectively captures the complex relationship between socioeconomic determinants and behavioral factors influencing global life expectancy. By integrating log transformations, the analysis achieves enhanced interpretability and precision, affirming the critical roles of economic development, equitable resource allocation, and targeted health behaviors in driving improved national health outcomes.

## Discussion and Conclusion
### Conclusions
This research provides an extensive and detailed analysis of critical socioeconomic and behavioral determinants affecting life expectancy through a robust linear regression framework. The analysis integrated several carefully transformed predictors, including the log-transformed income composition index, total health expenditure, alcohol consumption, and the square-root transformation of GDP, providing a nuanced understanding of global variations in health outcomes. The results clearly illustrate entrenched health inequalities between developing and developed nations, prominently highlighted by the StatusDeveloping variable, which showed a substantial negative coefficient (-2.1256, p-value < 0.001). This finding highlights persistent structural disparities and systemic challenges, highlighting that developing countries consistently experience lower life expectancy due to limitations in healthcare infrastructure, economic stability, and social resources.
Of the analyzed variables, log_Income emerged distinctly as the strongest predictor, exhibiting a significant positive association with life expectancy (coefficient 30.2937, p-value < 0.001). This critical insight emphasizes that economic prosperity and stability are essential foundations for improved public health outcomes, as higher income levels enable better access to quality healthcare, improved nutrition, increased educational opportunities, and overall enhanced living standards. Moreover, government healthcare expenditure significantly positively correlated with life expectancy (coefficient 0.74098, p-value < 0.001), confirming the crucial role of public health investments in enhancing population health outcomes through improved healthcare infrastructure, preventative services, and effective healthcare delivery systems.
Conversely, higher alcohol consumption negatively impacted life expectancy (coefficient -0.40239, p-value < 0.001), highlighting behavioral risks as significant detriments to health. This outcome underscores the need for effective public health interventions, including regulatory policies, educational campaigns, and community-based initiatives aimed at reducing alcohol-related harm. Collectively, these results validate the complexity and interconnected nature of life expectancy determinants, illustrating that comprehensive, multidimensional policy responses are essential to sustainably improve global health outcomes.
Key Findings and Supporting Evidence
This analysis identified four primary findings, each supported by robust statistical evidence. First, a consistent and significant negative effect associated with developing country status (StatusDeveloping, coefficient -2.1256, p-value < 0.001, 95% CI [-2.6926, -1.5587]) indicates deeply rooted systemic inequities that significantly disadvantage developing countries, resulting in reduced life expectancy compared to developed nations. These systemic challenges include limited access to quality healthcare, inadequate economic resources, and insufficient public health infrastructure.
Second, log_Income was identified as the most influential determinant, positively correlated with life expectancy (coefficient 30.2937, p-value < 0.001, 95% CI [29.3548, 31.2326]). The substantial magnitude of this effect emphasizes the critical role of economic stability and prosperity in enhancing public health outcomes. Increased income facilitates better healthcare access, higher nutritional standards, improved education, and overall enhanced living conditions, thereby directly influencing longevity.
Third, government health expenditure demonstrated a significant positive impact on life expectancy (coefficient 0.74098, p-value < 0.001, 95% CI [0.3520, 1.1300]). This result confirms that public investment in healthcare infrastructure, preventive care, and healthcare delivery significantly improves population health outcomes. Even moderate increases in government healthcare budgets can yield substantial improvements in life expectancy.
Fourth, alcohol consumption exhibited a strong negative relationship with life expectancy (coefficient -0.40239, p-value < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.4997, -0.3051]), reflecting substantial health risks associated with alcohol intake. This finding emphasizes the importance of effective behavioral interventions, including policy regulations, public awareness initiatives, and community programs aimed at reducing alcohol consumption and mitigating associated health risks.
Overall, the statistical robustness of the model (Adjusted R² = 0.79, F-statistic = 1666, p-value < 2.2e-16) reinforces its credibility and reliability, confirming its utility in guiding targeted public health policies and interventions aimed at enhancing global life expectancy.
Recommendations Based on Findings
The empirical findings of this study highlight the multifaceted nature of life expectancy and provide strong justification for targeted, evidence-based policy interventions. First and foremost, addressing the persistent gap in life expectancy between developed and developing countries necessitates long-term socioeconomic development. The significant negative coefficient of StatusDeveloping suggests that development status alone accounts for a considerable disadvantage. Governments and international institutions should therefore focus on increasing national income through equitable economic growth, expanding access to quality education, and promoting job creation. Structural investments in infrastructure and education systems, particularly in underdeveloped regions, would help uplift income indices, which—as evidenced by the large and statistically significant coefficient of log_Income—has the strongest influence on life expectancy.
Secondly, our findings show that even modest increases in government health expenditure (log_Expenditure) can lead to measurable improvements in population health. Policymakers, especially in low-income countries, should prioritize expanding health budgets to build robust primary care systems, ensure widespread vaccine coverage, and provide essential public health services. Strategic allocation of health funds toward prevention and early intervention programs can deliver substantial long-term benefits.
Thirdly, the significant negative impact of log_Alcohol on life expectancy highlights the urgent need for stronger public health measures around alcohol consumption. Governments should implement policies such as excise taxation, advertising restrictions, and community education programs to reduce harmful drinking behaviors. Culturally sensitive, grassroots-level interventions can be especially effective in communities with high alcohol prevalence.
Ultimately, improving life expectancy requires a holistic strategy that integrates socioeconomic, healthcare, and behavioral interventions. Policies must be tailored to country-specific contexts, ensuring that no determinant is addressed in isolation. A coordinated international response, rooted in statistical evidence, is vital to closing global health gaps and promoting sustainable human development.
Limitations and Future Improvements
While the final model demonstrates strong explanatory power and statistical significance, several limitations remain that future research should address to enhance robustness and applicability.
First, although the model achieved good fit statistics, it retained 4 outliers, 176 high-leverage points, and 150 influential observations, as flagged by diagnostic tools including standardized residuals, hat values, and Cook’s Distance. These observations were not removed because they represent real-world socioeconomic extremes—for instance, countries with unusually high GDP or low health expenditure—which are central to the study’s global context. However, their presence may still subtly affect coefficient estimates and residual variance. Future research could further examine the effect of such points through sensitivity analyses, or consider weighted regression techniques to lessen their impact without excluding valuable information.
Second, the model excluded potentially meaningful predictors such as BMI and Schooling due to a lack of statistical significance and poor diagnostic performance, even after transformation. While their exclusion was necessary to maintain model assumptions, it does limit the behavioral and educational dimensions of the analysis. Future models should explore a broader range of predictors, such as sanitation, healthcare access, air quality, and childhood mortality rates, to improve comprehensiveness. Including such variables may provide deeper insight into complex health determinants.
Third, the current model assumes additive and independent effects of predictors on life expectancy. However, many real-world factors likely interact—for example, the impact of income composition may depend on development status, or the influence of alcohol consumption could vary across income groups. Incorporating interaction terms in future models would allow for a more nuanced understanding of how these factors work together and under different conditions, revealing conditional effects that additive models may miss.
Lastly, this study is based on cross-sectional data, capturing life expectancy at a single point in time. Since life expectancy responds to long-term policy and structural changes, longitudinal or panel data would allow for a better understanding of causality and dynamic change. Tracking countries over time would help distinguish short-term noise from meaningful long-term relationships, offering stronger insights for global health policy design.
